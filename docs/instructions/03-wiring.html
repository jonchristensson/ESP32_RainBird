ESP32 Rain Sensor — Wiring Instructions

Connect the ESP32, relay module, and LM2596HV converter before bench testing and permanent installation.

1

Components

ESP32-WROOM-32U
Microcontroller with U.FL external antenna connector. Runs MicroPython firmware.
Relay module
Active-high, 3.3V-compatible signal. Screw terminals: VCC, GND, IN, NC, COM, NO.
LM2596HV buck converter
Adjustable AC/DC-to-DC module. Accepts AC 5–30V or DC 5–50V in, outputs adjustable 3.3–33V via an onboard trim pot — 3A rated, open PCB (not potted/sealed).
22AWG solid-core wire
Red for 5V, black for GND, yellow (or any) for signal, any for AC lines.
U.FL pigtail antenna
2.4GHz stick antenna with U.FL connector. Needed for reliable WiFi in locations with weak signal.
Heat shrink tubing
Cover every solder joint before moving to the next. No bare conductors.
Status LED (blue) + resistor
5mm blue LED on GPIO2 with a current-limiting resistor. Not onboard on this ESP32-WROOM-32U — status.py drives GPIO2 assuming a built-in LED that this board doesn't have, so one has to be added externally.
Relay module selection: Must be active-high (GPIO HIGH = energize) and accept 3.3V logic input. An active-low module inverts fail-open safety — the relay would energize on LOW, meaning a GPIO float or crash would block irrigation.
JD-VCC vs VCC: Some relay modules have two separate power pins — JD-VCC (coil power) and VCC (optocoupler power) — with a jumper between them. If yours has this, leave the jumper in place and connect your 5V wire to JD-VCC. The VCC pin then gets power through the jumper automatically.
This is not the sealed HLK-PM01 originally specced. The LM2596HV is an open-PCB, adjustable module — it ships with its trim pot set to an arbitrary voltage, not 5V. It must be manually calibrated with a multimeter before anything is connected to its output (Section 5, Phase 0). Because the pot isn't locked by default, vibration and heat cycling inside the enclosure can walk the voltage over time — lock the pot with a dot of glue or nail polish after calibrating, and spot-check the output voltage again during the 48-hour endurance test.
Status LED needs to be added externally. config.py's comment assumes an onboard LED on GPIO2, which this board doesn't have — confirmed during bench testing by directly toggling GPIO2 with a timer and watching for any board LED response (none). GPIO2 itself was verified to be switching correctly; there's simply nothing connected to it. Wiring for the added LED is in the table below (connections 10–11) and the diagram (Section 4).
2

Signal paths overview

There are three signal paths in this system. Understand these before running any wire.

Power path — AC to DC
Rain Bird 24VAC terminals → LM2596HV AC/DC input → LM2596HV 5V output (calibrated) → ESP32 VIN + relay VCC
Ground path — common bus
LM2596HV GND output → ESP32 GND + Relay GND
Control path — firmware to Rain Bird
ESP32 GPIO 22 → Relay IN ⟹ Relay NC/COM → Rain Bird SEN terminals
Fail-open by design: The relay NC (normally closed) terminal is wired to the Rain Bird SEN circuit. When the relay is de-energized — whether because there is no rain, the ESP32 has no power, or the ESP32 has lost WiFi — the NC circuit stays closed and irrigation proceeds normally. The relay only energizes (opens the NC circuit, blocking irrigation) when the firmware actively drives GPIO 22 HIGH because rain thresholds are exceeded.

Connection-by-connection wiring table

Make these connections in order. Locate the Rain Bird terminals (Section 5), calibrate the converter (Section 6), then bench-test (Section 7) before soldering permanently.

Finding pins on the ESP32: Pin labels are printed on the board's silkscreen — look for VIN, GND, and 22 (GPIO 22). On most ESP32-WROOM-32U dev boards, VIN and GND are at the top of one rail, and GPIO 22 is near the top of the opposite rail. If in doubt, search the exact board name + "pinout" — the silkscreen label is always authoritative.
# Wire From To Notes
1 Red 22AWG LM2596HV Vo+ (5V, post-calibration) terminal ESP32 VIN pin 5VDC supply to ESP32
2 Red 22AWG LM2596HV Vo+ terminal (or daisy-chain from ESP32 VIN) Relay module VCC screw terminal 5VDC supply to relay
3 Black 22AWG LM2596HV Vo− (GND) terminal ESP32 GND pin Common ground — daisy-chain to relay GND in next step
4 Black 22AWG ESP32 GND pin (or LM2596HV Vo− terminal) Relay module GND screw terminal Completes common ground bus
5 Signal 22AWG ESP32 GPIO 22 pin Relay module IN screw terminal HIGH = energize relay (block irrigation)
6 Any 22AWG LM2596HV AC/DC input terminal 1 (labeled L, AC1, or IN+ depending on your board — check silkscreen) Rain Bird 24VAC terminal 1 (free end for now) AC line — polarity doesn't matter for 24VAC
7 Any 22AWG LM2596HV AC/DC input terminal 2 (labeled N, AC2, or IN− depending on your board) Rain Bird 24VAC terminal 2 (free end for now) AC neutral — polarity doesn't matter for 24VAC
8 Any 22AWG Relay module NC screw terminal Rain Bird SEN terminal 1 (free end for now) Normally-closed side of relay — circuit is closed when relay is off
9 Any 22AWG Relay module COM screw terminal Rain Bird SEN terminal 2 (free end for now) Common side of relay
10 Signal 22AWG ESP32 GPIO 2 pin Resistor (330Ω) → LED anode (long leg) Status LED signal — HIGH lights it (matches status.py's active-high logic)
11 Black 22AWG LED cathode (short leg, flat side of LED body) ESP32 GND pin (or GND bus) Completes the LED circuit
Connections 6–9 are left with free ends during bench testing. Only connect the Rain Bird terminals (24VAC and SEN) during the final installation step (issue 08).

Locating the Rain Bird connections

Before wiring anything to the controller, find and identify the low-voltage terminal strip. This is separate from the numbered station terminals (1, 2, 3… one per irrigation zone) — look for a smaller block near the transformer input, usually behind the controller's front door/cover.

Power down before touching any terminal. Even though this is low-voltage (24VAC, not mains), switch off the breaker or unplug the transformer before connecting, disconnecting, or probing wires at this strip. This protects the controller's internal electronics from an accidental short while you're working, not just you.

What this unit's strip looks like

On this ESP-ME, the low-voltage terminal strip has 5 screw terminals in a row:

  • Terminals 1–2, labeled 24 VOLT — the transformer secondary. This is the AC input for the LM2596HV converter (connections 6–7). Polarity doesn't matter, it's AC.
  • Terminal 3, labeled GND — chassis/earth ground, unrelated to our power or signal path. Leave it alone; nothing in this project connects here.
  • Terminals 4–5, unlabeled on the silkscreen — these are the sensor bypass (SEN) terminals. Confirmed by the wires already landed there: this is where the old, broken rain sensor is currently connected. This is where the relay's NC/COM wires go (connections 8–9).
Rain Bird's own documentation calls these two unlabeled terminals SENS or SEN, and on some ESP-Me revisions they ship with a yellow jumper wire across them (bypassing the sensor circuit so the controller runs unrestricted with no sensor installed) — that's the pair the old rain sensor now occupies. If you ever see a bare jumper wire instead of a sensor here, that's the same pair.

Removing the old sensor

With power off, disconnect the two wires currently on terminals 4–5. Keep track of which physical wires they were — you won't reuse them, but it confirms you have the right terminal pair before you land the relay's NC/COM wires there in issue 08.

Once you can point to "24 VOLT x2, GND, and the two SEN terminals with the old sensor wires," you have everything located. Proceed to converter calibration (Section 6) — that step doesn't require being at the Rain Bird, so it can be done at your desk first.
6

Phase 0 — Converter calibration (at desk, before wiring)

The LM2596HV ships with its trim pot set to an arbitrary voltage — not 5V. Do this before the module is ever connected to the ESP32 or relay; feeding the wrong voltage into ESP32 VIN can damage the board.

No load connected during calibration. Do not have the ESP32 or relay wired to the converter's output while you adjust the pot — set the voltage first, on an unloaded output, then connect the load afterward and re-check.

Powering the converter for calibration

The LM2596HV accepts AC 5–30V or DC 5–50V on its input — you don't need to be at the Rain Bird for this step. Any of these work fine as a bench input source:

  • A 9V battery (this is exactly the kind of test a 9V battery is good for on this module — unlike the old HLK-PM01 plan, this converter takes DC input)
  • A spare 12V or similar DC wall adapter
  • Any small AC transformer in the 5–30VAC range, if you have one

Setting the output voltage

  • Connect your chosen input source to the converter's input terminals
  • Multimeter in DC voltage mode on the output terminals (Vo+/Vo−), nothing else connected to the output
  • Turn the trim pot slowly (small screwdriver) while watching the multimeter
  • Stop at 5.0V ±0.1V
  • Let it sit powered for a few minutes, re-check — cheap pots can drift slightly as the board warms up. Re-adjust if needed

Locking the pot

  • Once stable at 5.0V, put a small dot of glue or nail polish on the trim pot to keep it from turning under vibration once mounted permanently
  • Let it cure before moving on

Verify under load

  • With the pot now locked, connect the ESP32 (and relay, once wired) to the output
  • Re-check the output voltage with the real load attached — it should stay within 4.8–5.2V. If it sags below 4.8V under load, the input source may be too weak (a 9V battery under real load current can sag — swap to a wall adapter if so) rather than a calibration problem
Phase 0 complete? Proceed to Phase 1 (Section 7) — USB bench test with the calibrated converter now in the circuit.
7

Phase 1 — USB bench test (at desk)

Make connections 1–5 only (DC side). The LM2596HV sits in the circuit, already calibrated to 5V (Section 6), but is not yet the power source — the ESP32 runs from USB as normal. Leave connections 6–9 (AC input and SEN lines) with free ends.

This phase catches relay wiring mistakes before anything is soldered. The converter's AC input (tapped from the Rain Bird's real 24VAC) is verified in Phase 2.

This section assumes MicroPython is already flashed and secrets.py is already configured on this ESP32 (it was, for laptop bring-up). Full USB/serial basics — installing mpremote, finding your COM port, flashing from scratch — are in 02-flash-guide.html if you need them.

Before you start

  • mpremote installed on your PC (pip install mpremote if not — one-time)
  • Your COM port (Device Manager → Ports, or the PowerShell one-liner in 02-flash-guide.html Step 1a)
  • Your OTA_PASSWORD from secrets.py — needed for the config pushes below
  • tools/webrepl_cli.py is already in the project — no download needed

1. Power on and watch the boot

  • Plug the ESP32 into your laptop with a USB data cable — this powers it for Phase 1, the converter isn't providing power yet
  • Open serial: mpremote connect COM4 repl (replace COM4 with your port)
  • Press EN on the ESP32 to force a clean boot and watch the startup messages
  • Confirm: WiFi connects and prints an IP address — write it down, you need it for the OTA pushes below — a weather check runs, and status reaches "ok" (or "rain" if there's genuinely rain in the forecast right now)
  • Leave the device running — don't reset or unplug yet

2. Baseline relay continuity

  • Multimeter continuity mode across the relay's NC and COM screw terminals (connections 8–9, free ends)
  • Should beep (closed) — relay is de-energized because thresholds aren't currently exceeded

3. Force a block and confirm the relay opens

  • Exit the REPL with Ctrl+] — this only detaches your serial session, the device keeps running
  • On your PC, edit your local config.py: set RAIN_THRESHOLD_6H = -1.0 — use a negative value, not 0.0. The comparison in irrigation.py is strict (rain_6h > threshold), and rain_6h is often exactly 0.000 on a dry day, so 0.0 won't reliably force a block
  • Push it over WiFi from the project root: python tools/webrepl_cli.py -p <OTA_PASSWORD> config.py <device_ip>:config.py (use the IP from step 1)
  • Reboot to apply it — press EN on the board, or run mpremote connect COM4 reset
  • Reopen serial (mpremote connect COM4 repl) — you should hear the relay click and see "Irrigation: BLOCKING…" in the output
  • Multimeter on NC/COM: should now show open (no continuity)

4. Prove fail-open overrides a block — the safety-critical test

This is the test that actually matters: even while the relay is actively blocking irrigation, losing WiFi must release it. Do this before restoring the threshold, while the relay is still energized from step 3 — testing WiFi loss after the relay is already de-energized wouldn't prove anything.

  • With the relay still energized, disconnect WiFi — turn off your router, or temporarily change its SSID
  • Within about 10 seconds, serial should show "WiFi: connection lost..." followed by "Status: ... -> error"
  • Multimeter on NC/COM: should return to closed (continuity) — the relay de-energized automatically despite the rain threshold still being force-blocked
  • LED should be fast-blinking (error state)
  • Restore WiFi (turn the router back on / revert the SSID change) — serial shows "WiFi: reconnected", and the relay re-energizes again on the next check cycle since the threshold is still 0.0

5. Restore normal thresholds

  • Edit your local config.py back to RAIN_THRESHOLD_6H = 0.25 and RAIN_THRESHOLD_12H = 0.5
  • Push it — same tools/webrepl_cli.py command as step 3
  • Reboot, confirm the relay de-energizes (click), and the multimeter on NC/COM beeps again (closed)

6. Total power loss — simplest fail-open check

  • Unplug USB from the ESP32 entirely
  • Multimeter on NC/COM: stays closed — no power at all means the relay coil can't be energized, so the NC contact is closed by default

LED states (reference)

This ESP32-WROOM-32U has no onboard LED on GPIO2 — status.py assumes one exists, but it doesn't on this board. Wire an external blue LED + 330Ω resistor on GPIO2 (connections 10–11 in the wiring table, Section 3) to see these patterns. Confirmed during bench testing: GPIO2 toggles correctly (verified with a direct timer test in the REPL), there's just nothing connected to it without this addition.
  • Solid on — weather check succeeded, thresholds not exceeded ("ok")
  • Slow blink — rain thresholds exceeded, relay energized ("rain")
  • Fast blink — error state (WiFi down, API failure)
Phase 1 complete? Proceed to Phase 2 (Section 8) — converter verification at the Rain Bird before soldering.
8

Phase 2 — Converter verification (at Rain Bird, before mounting)

Bring the wired assembly (still loose, not soldered) to the Rain Bird enclosure — the terminals you need were identified in Section 5. Connect the AC lines temporarily to confirm the already-calibrated LM2596HV still outputs 5V from the real 24VAC source and can power the ESP32, before committing to permanent mounting.

The Rain Bird's 24VAC is low-voltage AC, not mains. It is not directly dangerous at these voltages, but treat exposed wires carefully — don't touch bare conductors while the Rain Bird is powered.

Converter voltage check

  • Disconnect USB from ESP32
  • Connect LM2596HV AC input wires (connections 6–7) to the Rain Bird 24 VOLT terminals — polarity doesn't matter
  • Power on the Rain Bird — set multimeter to DC voltage, probe LM2596HV output terminals
  • Reading should still be 4.8–5.2V DC, matching the desk calibration in Section 6. If it's drifted outside that range, re-check the trim pot before proceeding

Converter-powered boot

  • With the converter running, connect your laptop to the ESP32 via USB data cable (for serial only — the ESP32 will draw power from the converter, not USB)
  • Open serial: mpremote connect COM3 repl, press EN to reboot
  • Serial shows normal boot: WiFi connects, weather check runs, logging row appears in Google Sheet
  • No errors — LED reaches solid "ok" state
If the ESP32 draws power from USB instead of the converter when both are connected, that is fine — the important check is that the converter outputs the correct voltage. The ESP32 will run from converter power alone once USB is disconnected after soldering.
Phase 2 complete? Proceed to the WiFi signal test (Section 9) while you have the device at the Rain Bird location.
9

External antenna & WiFi signal test

The Rain Bird controller may be in a garage, utility closet, outdoor enclosure, or another location with weak WiFi signal. The ESP32-WROOM-32U has a U.FL connector specifically for an external antenna in this scenario.

Connecting the antenna

This is a snap-fit connector, not a soldered or screw connection — which is why it's not in the wiring table above. Still worth doing carefully, since U.FL connectors are small and easy to damage.

  • Find the U.FL port: a small round metal receptacle directly on the ESP32-WROOM-32U module itself (not the dev board), near the edge where the module's shielding can is
  • Align the pigtail's connector straight down over the port — no angle
  • Press straight down until it clicks. It shouldn't take much force; if it feels like it's not seating, re-check alignment rather than pushing harder
  • No jumper or resistor to move — the WROOM-32U variant routes RF exclusively through the U.FL port, there's no onboard trace antenna competing with it
Never pull the cable to disconnect it. U.FL connectors detach by prying up on the connector body itself (fingernail or a plastic spudger against the metal shell), not by tugging the wire — pulling the cable can rip the connector off the pigtail or damage the port on the module.

Route the antenna cable so the stick antenna can be positioned outside or near the edge of the Rain Bird enclosure for best signal. Avoid tight coils right at the connector (strain on the joint) and avoid running the cable alongside the 24VAC or AC input wiring — keep some separation to reduce noise coupling into the RF line. A dab of hot glue or tape a few inches back from the connector can relieve strain without stressing the U.FL joint itself.

Signal test

With the antenna attached and the ESP32 at the Rain Bird location (from Phase 2 above), check the RSSI reported in serial output on boot, or read it from the Google Sheet after a check cycle runs. Target: above −70 dBm. Below −75 dBm, reposition the antenna or consider a WiFi extender before proceeding.

RSSI is logged to the Google Sheet on every check cycle once logging is running — you can monitor it remotely after installation.
10

Permanent soldering checklist

Only solder once Phase 1, Phase 2, and the WiFi signal test are all complete. Final firmware must be flashed via USB before soldering — USB access is impractical after.

Flash firmware before soldering. Once the assembly is permanent, OTA (WebREPL) is the only update path. Verify OTA works end-to-end (issue 04) before making any connections permanent.

Pre-soldering prerequisites

  • Issues 01–03 firmware is flashed and verified (logging works)
  • OTA verified working (issue 04)
  • Converter calibrated to 5.0V and pot locked (Section 6 above)
  • Phase 1 bench test passed (Section 7 above)
  • Phase 2 converter verification passed (Section 8 above)
  • WiFi signal test passed at Rain Bird location (Section 9 above)

Soldering order

  • Solder connection 1: LM2596HV Vo+ → ESP32 VIN. Apply heat shrink before moving on.
  • Solder connection 2: LM2596HV Vo+ → Relay VCC. Apply heat shrink.
  • Solder connection 3: LM2596HV Vo− → ESP32 GND. Apply heat shrink.
  • Solder connection 4: GND bus → Relay GND. Apply heat shrink.
  • Solder connection 5: ESP32 GPIO 22 → Relay IN. Apply heat shrink.
  • Solder connections 10–11: ESP32 GPIO 2 → resistor → LED anode, LED cathode → GND bus. Apply heat shrink.
  • Crimp or tin connections 6–9 (AC input, SEN lines) — leave free ends long enough to reach Rain Bird terminals.
The status LED won't be visible once the assembly is sealed inside the Rain Bird enclosure — it's included here because it's useful for bench testing and any future desk debugging, not because it serves a purpose after final install. Solder it in anyway if you want it available for next time you have the enclosure open; skip it if you'd rather not bother since Google Sheets logging already covers post-install visibility.

Final inspection before installation

  • Visual check: every solder joint has heat shrink, no exposed metal
  • Tug test: pull each wire at each joint — nothing should move
  • Power up the complete assembly one final time — firmware runs, WiFi connects, weather check succeeds, logging row appears in Google Sheet
